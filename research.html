Ali Parchekani

Research

My research asks how a reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS) can improve the accuracy and reliability of wireless sensing under practical constraints. A RIS is a panel of small elements. The reflection of each element can be set electronically, so the panel can change how a radio signal reaches a scene and comes back.

I am a PhD candidate in the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the University of Toronto. My supervisor is Professor Shahrokh Valaee.

Thesis
Sensing and Scanning Using Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces The thesis has three parts: choosing configurations that make a scene easier to estimate, keeping a radar target when other radars interfere, and understanding when surface control can mislead a receiver. The complete draft is with my supervisor, and I plan to defend in Winter 2027.

Configuring the surface to sense a scene

The problem

A RIS with 16 elements and 4 states per element can form 416 different configurations. Only a few of them can be used in one sensing task, so the question is which ones to use. Increasing the received power is one useful goal, but the estimator also needs measurements that are linearly independent.

What I did

I score a set of configurations with the Cramér–Rao bound on the estimate of the scene. To search for a good set I use the Swendsen-Wang algorithm. Neighbouring elements that are in the same state are bonded into clusters with some probability, and each cluster changes its state together. Several copies of the search run with different bond probabilities and share the best configuration they find.

This method needs no training, in contrast to a reinforcement learning policy. After the measurements are taken, least squares recovers the reflection of each cell, and a classifier labels each cell as empty, cube or sphere.

Result

In the synthetic recognition study, 97.24% of 8,000 held-out cells were labelled correctly. I compared the search with reinforcement learning, simulated annealing and sampling methods, and I checked the estimator against its theoretical bound over 10,000 noise trials.

About 97 of every 100 held-out cells are labelled correctly.

Keeping the target when other radars interfere

The problem

Automotive radars share the same band. When the radar of another car transmits on the same channel, its signal can hide a weak target after range-Doppler processing. A RIS in front of the receiver can change how the target signal and the interference add up before they are processed.

Most designs assume that each element of the surface acts alone. In a real surface the current on one element induces a voltage on its neighbours. This is called mutual coupling, and a design that ignores it does not behave as planned.

What I did

I model the surface as a coupled network and choose one set of loads that stays fixed for the whole OFDM waveform. The design keeps the response to the target above a floor and minimizes the interference, on average and in the worst case.

I then make the same design without the coupling terms and compare the two. Both are evaluated on the coupled model, on every subcarrier, and after range-Doppler processing and detection.

Result

At the reference operating point, the design that accounts for coupling has a 13.33 dB higher worst-case carrier SINR. It is better on 107 of the 109 subcarriers. The two subcarriers at the upper edge of the band go the other way. These are simulation results.

The 109 subcarriers in order of frequency. The filled ones are where the coupling-aware design is better.

When a surface can mislead a radar

The problem

The control that helps sensing can also be used against it. If a vehicle carries a RIS, can it make a radar report a target at a wrong bearing and with a wrong velocity?

What I did

I study a vehicle with a surface in front of an automotive OFDM radar. The surface changes its reflection from one symbol to the next, and the radar then reports a target at a false bearing and a false velocity. The range is not changed. An OFDM radar measures the delay from the phase across its subcarriers, and the surface does not change that.

I compare two types of receiver. An analog receiver scans its beams one after another. A digital receiver keeps the signals of all its antennas. I also look at how the rule a receiver uses to report detections changes which false targets it accepts.

Result

The study is in simulation and at the level of the radar measurement, before tracking. The conference paper was accepted at IEEE ISAC 2026. It is joint work with H. Chen, H. Wymeersch and S. Valaee. The journal paper with the comparison of receivers is in preparation.
